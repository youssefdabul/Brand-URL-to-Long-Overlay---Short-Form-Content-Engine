# Long overlay — hook patterns

One block of text over a single clip. Read silently in under eight seconds. No payoff coming, no CTA, no second line. The text *is* the video.

---

## What makes this format work

The emotion is **recognition**, not curiosity. A spoken hook opens a loop the video closes. This closes nothing — it names something so precisely that the viewer feels caught, and the comment section becomes the payoff.

Five rules underneath all the patterns below:

**25–45 words.** Under 25 and it's a caption. Over 45 and nobody finishes reading before they've scrolled.

**End on the sharpest concrete noun.** Not a conclusion, not a lesson. The last three words are what gets screenshotted.

**One true detail minimum.** "40 tabs open" not "disorganised". "the 2:47am email" not "late at night". Specificity is the entire mechanic — vagueness reads as written-by-committee.

**The clip must not illustrate the text.** If the text is about burnout and the clip is someone looking tired, you've made an advert. Deadpan, unrelated, or contradicting is what makes it land. A person staring blankly while the text describes their inner collapse.

**All lowercase, no emojis, no exclamation marks.** The register is someone typing quietly, not a brand announcing something.

---

## The patterns

### 1. The forensic callout
Name a type of person, then accumulate details until it's uncomfortably specific. Each clause narrows.

> the guy who says he's "exploring options" but has the same three job boards open in pinned tabs and hasn't changed his cv since march

Works because the reader recognises themselves somewhere in the accumulation. Stop one detail past comfortable.

---

### 2. Compressed timeline
Two moments in one sentence, with the gap doing the work. No transition, no explanation.

> you were the smartest person in your sixth form and now you're refreshing an application portal at 1am hoping a company you can't spell rejects you politely

The jump is the joke and the grief at once.

---

### 3. Unspoken internal monologue
What someone is thinking and would never say out loud. Second person.

> you're not tired you just don't want to open the document because opening it means finding out how far behind you actually are

Best of the set for getting saves rather than likes. People send these to each other.

---

### 4. The inventory
A list of possessions, habits or tabs that paints a whole person without naming them.

> linkedin premium, a notion template you paid for, three half-finished courses, and a spotify playlist called "lock in" that you've listened to twice

Comma rhythm matters more than content here. Four items, the fourth one funniest.

---

### 5. Everyone but you
Social comparison stated flatly, never complained about. The flatness is what stops it being self-pity.

> everyone you graduated with has a linkedin banner saying "excited to announce" and you have a spreadsheet called applications_final_v3

---

### 6. The delayed understanding
Something said years ago that only makes sense now. Opens with a time marker.

> your dad said "you'll understand when you're older" and you thought he meant taxes but he meant this specific feeling on a sunday evening

Softer register. Good when the clip is quiet and the audio is sad piano rather than meme-chaotic.

---

### 7. The conditional trap
"if you… you already know" — makes the reader self-select into the video.

> if you've ever rewritten the same opening line of a cold email eleven times and then not sent it, you already know exactly what's wrong and it isn't the email

---

### 8. The reframe
A flat correction of something the reader believes about themselves. No advice attached.

> you're not bad at networking you just can't bring yourself to ask a stranger for something when you're not sure you deserve it yet

---

## For brand work

Same patterns, one extra constraint: **the brand appears exactly once, as a verb phrase, never in the first line.**

"speedrunning flashcards on youlearn" is correct. "youlearn helps you study faster" is an advert and the format rejects it — the text has to read as something a person typed, and people don't recommend products mid-thought.

The brand is what the character in the text is *doing*, not what the text is *about*.

> spent four hours making a revision timetable, zero hours revising, and now it's 11pm and you're speedrunning flashcards on youlearn like that was the plan all along

---

## Where this format breaks

Long overlay suits software, AI tools, apps and anything with a relatable failure state. It struggles with lifestyle and physical products — Monzo or Oura don't have an obvious "person failing at something" frame, and forcing one produces the generic version. Those want carousels or demo-first formats instead.

Worth knowing before you pitch it as the answer to everything.
